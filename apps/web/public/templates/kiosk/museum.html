The Twilight Zone 200–1,000 m Where the last of the sunlight disappears and the largest animal migration on Earth begins every night. STATION 1 — THE TWILIGHT ZONE Between 200 and 1,000 metres, sunlight fades from blue dusk to total black. This is the mesopelagic, or 'twilight,' zone — a layer so vast it may hold more fish biomass than the rest of the ocean combined. Every night, billions of lanternfish, shrimp, and squid rise hundreds of metres toward the surface to feed under cover of darkness, then sink back down before dawn. It is the largest synchronized migration of animals on the planet, and it moves carbon from the surface to the deep with every trip. The daily vertical migration was first mistaken for a 'false sea floor' by WWII sonar operators — a phantom layer that rose and fell with the sun. Living Light Bioluminescence In a world without sun, most animals make their own light — to hunt, to hide, and to find a mate. STATION 2 — BIOLUMINESCENCE An estimated 76% of deep-sea animals produce their own light through a chemical reaction between a molecule called luciferin and oxygen. The result is cold light — almost no heat — in blues and greens that travel farthest through seawater. Light is a tool of survival. Anglerfish dangle a glowing lure; some squid cloak their silhouette with belly lights to vanish against the faint glow above; others release a blinding cloud of luminous mucus and disappear into the dark. The light of a single firefly squid is bright enough to read by — and millions of them gather each spring to turn Toyama Bay electric blue. The Giant Squid Architeuthis dux A legend made real — the largest invertebrate eye in the animal kingdom, filmed alive for the first time in 2012. STATION 3 — THE GIANT SQUID For centuries the giant squid lived only in sailors' tales of the kraken. It was not filmed alive in its natural habitat until 2012, when a team lured one before a camera 630 metres down using a glowing decoy. Its eyes are the size of dinner plates — the largest of any animal — tuned to catch the faintest flicker of light, perhaps the glow of its only true predator, the sperm whale, disturbing bioluminescent plankton as it dives. Sperm whales surface with circular scars from squid suckers — the only evidence of battles that take place a kilometre below, in total darkness. Hydrothermal Vents Chemosynthesis Volcanic chimneys on the sea floor feed entire ecosystems with chemicals instead of sunlight. STATION 4 — HYDROTHERMAL VENTS Discovered only in 1977, hydrothermal vents are cracks in the sea floor where seawater, superheated by magma, erupts at up to 400°C, rich in minerals. Around them thrives life that needs no sun at all. Bacteria here perform chemosynthesis — building food from hydrogen sulfide instead of light. They feed towering tube worms, ghostly crabs, and shrimp, in oases of life on an otherwise barren plain. Many scientists believe life on Earth may have begun in exactly such a place. Vent tube worms have no mouth or gut — they are fed entirely by billions of bacteria living inside their bodies. Whale Fall An ecosystem from death When a whale dies and sinks, it becomes an island of life that can feed the deep for fifty years. STATION 5 — WHALE FALL When a great whale dies, its body sinks to the sea floor and becomes a 'whale fall' — a sudden feast in a food desert. Sharks and hagfish strip the flesh within months; then worms, snails, and crustaceans move in for years. Finally, bacteria break down the lipids locked in the bones, releasing sulfides that support vent-like communities. A single whale can sustain a thriving ecosystem for up to half a century — and some species are known to live nowhere else. The bone-eating worm Osedax has no mouth and no stomach — it drills into whale bone with body acid and digests the fat inside. Into the Abyss Exploration & technology More people have walked on the Moon than have reached the deepest point of the ocean. STATION 6 — EXPLORING THE ABYSS The Challenger Deep, nearly 11 kilometres down, has been visited by fewer than two dozen people. The pressure there is over a thousand times that at the surface — enough to crush an unprotected submarine like a soda can. Today, remotely operated vehicles and autonomous robots map the dark with sonar and bring back samples and footage. Yet over 80% of the ocean floor remains unmapped in detail — making the deep sea the largest unexplored frontier on our own planet. We have higher-resolution maps of Mars and the Moon than we do of our own ocean floor.
